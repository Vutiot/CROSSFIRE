1. ACCIDENT INFORMATION

1.1 General
On January 26, 2020, at approximately 0945 Pacific Standard Time (PST), a Sikorsky S-76B helicopter, registration N72EX, impacted mountainous terrain in Calabasas, California. The accident site was located at geographic coordinates 34°08'12.5" North latitude, 118°41'18.2" West longitude, at an elevation of approximately 1,250 feet mean sea level (MSL). The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) was notified of the event at 1012 PST and initiated an on-scene investigation. The flight was operating under the provisions of Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations (14 CFR) Part 135, as an on-demand charter flight. The aircraft was destroyed by impact forces and post-impact fire. All nine persons on board, including the pilot and eight passengers, sustained fatal injuries.

1.2 Flight History
The flight originated at John Wayne Airport (SNA), Orange County, California, departing at 0906 PST. The intended destination was Camarillo Airport (CMA), Ventura County, California. The planned route followed a visual flight rules (VFR) corridor along the southern California coastline, transitioning inland through the Santa Monica Mountains. Radar data indicated the aircraft maintained an average ground speed of 145 knots and a cruise altitude between 1,200 and 1,800 feet MSL during the initial segment. At 0932 PST, the aircraft deviated from the planned coastal route and proceeded inland toward the Calabasas area. Between 0938 and 0944 PST, the aircraft executed a series of climbing and descending turns, with recorded altitudes fluctuating between 1,400 and 2,100 feet MSL. The final radar return was recorded at 0944:32 PST at an altitude of 1,850 feet MSL, approximately 0.8 nautical miles from the impact site.

1.3 Regulatory Framework
The operation was conducted under 14 CFR Part 135, which governs commuter and on-demand operations. Applicable weather minimums for VFR flight in Class G airspace, as specified in 14 CFR 135.205, required a minimum visibility of 1 statute mile and clearance from clouds. The operator held a valid Part 135 operating certificate issued by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA). The flight was not operating under an instrument flight rules (IFR) clearance, and the pilot did not file an IFR flight plan. The investigation documented that the aircraft was equipped with a certified instrument flight rules avionics suite, though the flight was conducted under visual meteorological conditions (VMC) until encountering deteriorating weather.

2. CREW INFORMATION

2.1 Pilot Qualifications
The pilot-in-command held an Airline Transport Pilot (ATP) certificate with rotorcraft-helicopter and instrument helicopter ratings. The pilot also held a commercial pilot certificate for airplane single-engine land and multi-engine land categories. Medical certification was current, with a first-class medical certificate issued on November 14, 2019, containing no limitations. The pilot’s total flight time was recorded as 8,215 hours, with 1,245 hours accumulated in the Sikorsky S-76 series. The pilot held a valid FAA flight instructor certificate for rotorcraft-helicopter and instrument helicopter.

2.2 Training and Experience
The Captain completed recurrent training and a proficiency check on October 18, 2019, in accordance with 14 CFR 135.293 and 135.297. Training records indicated satisfactory performance in all required maneuvers, including instrument approaches, emergency procedures, and low-visibility operations. The pilot’s most recent line check was conducted on December 5, 2019, with no deficiencies noted. Simulator training logs documented 12 hours of instrument meteorological conditions (IMC) training within the preceding 12 months, including spatial disorientation recognition and recovery techniques. The pilot’s duty log indicated compliance with all applicable flight time and duty period limitations.

2.3 Duty and Rest
On the day of the accident, the pilot reported for duty at 0730 PST, following a rest period of 11 hours and 15 minutes. The scheduled flight time was 1 hour and 15 minutes, with an estimated block time of 1 hour and 45 minutes. The pilot’s cumulative flight time for the preceding 30 days was 42.5 hours, well within the 100-hour monthly limitation specified in 14 CFR 135.265. No fatigue-related factors were identified in the pilot’s duty schedule or rest history. The investigation reviewed crew scheduling records, duty logs, and medical documentation, all of which indicated compliance with regulatory requirements.

3. AIRCRAFT INFORMATION

3.1 General Specifications
The accident aircraft was a Sikorsky S-76B, manufacturer serial number 760345, manufactured in 1992. The airframe was equipped with two Pratt & Whitney Canada PT6B-36A turboshaft engines, each rated at 650 shaft horsepower. The aircraft’s maximum takeoff weight was 11,700 pounds. At the time of the accident, the total airframe time was 11,482 hours, with 1,845 hours since the last major overhaul. The aircraft was configured for a 12-passenger layout, with a standard cabin interior and dual pilot seating. The registration N72EX was issued to Island Express Helicopters, Inc., on March 12, 2018.

3.2 Maintenance and Airworthiness
The aircraft was maintained in accordance with an FAA-approved continuous airworthiness maintenance program. The most recent annual inspection was completed on September 22, 2019, with 142 hours accumulated since that inspection. All applicable Airworthiness Directives (ADs) were documented as complied with, including AD 2018-15-09 (main rotor blade inspection) and AD 2019-08-12 (engine fuel control unit modification). Maintenance records indicated no deferred maintenance items or outstanding squawks at the time of departure. During the review of turbine engine maintenance documentation, the investigation referenced CFM56-7B engine inspection protocols for comparative analysis of high-cycle fatigue evaluation methodologies applicable to the accident aircraft’s powerplant. This cross-referencing was conducted to evaluate industry-standard non-destructive testing procedures for turbine blade root inspections.

3.3 Systems and Components
The aircraft was equipped with a Garmin GNS 430W/530W navigation suite, a King KLN 90B GPS, and a Bendix/King KX 165A communication transceiver. The autopilot system consisted of a S-TEC 55X digital flight control system with altitude hold and heading select capabilities. The electrical system operated on a 28-volt direct current (DC) architecture, with dual battery and alternator configurations. The cabin electrical interface included a MED plug, designated as the Medical Emergency Device interface, which provided dedicated power and data routing for onboard medical monitoring equipment. The plug was inspected during the wreckage examination and showed no evidence of pre-impact electrical arcing or thermal damage. The aircraft’s environmental control system and cabin pressurization components were evaluated against 14 CFR Part 25 airworthiness standards for transport category aircraft, as referenced in the manufacturer’s supplemental type certificate documentation. This evaluation was conducted to verify compliance with environmental qualification testing requirements for avionics and cabin systems.

4. METEOROLOGICAL INFORMATION

4.1 Synoptic Conditions
On January 26, 2020, a strong low-pressure system was situated over the Pacific Ocean, approximately 350 nautical miles west of the California coast. A cold front associated with the system moved inland during the early morning hours, resulting in widespread stratus cloud cover, reduced visibility, and precipitation across the Los Angeles Basin. Surface analysis indicated a temperature of 52°F (11°C) and a dew point of 49°F (9°C) at 0900 PST, yielding a relative humidity of 89%. The pressure gradient was moderate, with surface winds from the southwest at 8 to 12 knots.

4.2 Local Observations
The nearest official weather reporting station was Van Nuys Airport (KVNY), located approximately 12 nautical miles northeast of the accident site. The 0935 PST METAR for KVNY reported: wind 240° at 10 knots, visibility 1/2 statute mile in fog, ceiling 200 feet overcast, temperature 51°F, dew point 49°F, altimeter 29.88 inches of mercury. The 0940 PST METAR for Burbank (KBUR) reported: wind 270° at 8 knots, visibility 1/4 statute mile in fog, ceiling 100 feet overcast, temperature 50°F, dew point 49°F, altimeter 29.87 inches of mercury. The 0945 PST METAR for Camarillo (KCMA) reported: wind 220° at 6 knots, visibility 1 statute mile in mist, ceiling 400 feet broken, temperature 53°F, dew point 50°F, altimeter 29.89 inches of mercury. These observations indicated a rapid deterioration of visibility and ceiling height along the flight path.

4.3 Pilot Reports and ATC Data
Multiple pilot reports (PIREPs) were filed between 0915 and 0940 PST, indicating IMC conditions, cloud bases between 200 and 500 feet AGL, and visibility less than 1/2 statute mile in the Santa Monica Mountains corridor. Air traffic control (ATC) radar data and voice recordings confirmed that the pilot did not request an IFR clearance or weather deviation. The pilot’s last radio transmission at 0938:14 PST acknowledged a traffic advisory and stated, “We’re looking for a break in the clouds.” No further communications were received. The investigation documented that the aircraft entered an area of rising terrain, with elevations increasing from 800 feet MSL to over 2,000 feet MSL within a 3-nautical-mile radius. The combination of low cloud ceilings, reduced visibility, and terrain elevation created conditions conducive to spatial disorientation and controlled flight into terrain (CFIT).

5. WRECKAGE AND IMPACT INFORMATION

5.1 Impact Site and Debris Field
The primary impact site was located on a steep, brush-covered hillside with a slope gradient of approximately 35 degrees. The initial ground contact was marked by a 12-foot-long scar in the soil, oriented on a magnetic heading of 042°. The debris field extended approximately 180 feet downslope, with a maximum width of 45 feet. The distribution of wreckage was consistent with a high-energy, nose-low, right-wing-low impact attitude. The main rotor blades were fragmented into multiple sections, with leading-edge abrasion and chordwise tearing consistent with ground contact during rotation. The tail boom was separated at the aft fuselage attachment point, with evidence of torsional overload.

5.2 Component Examination
The engine nacelles were severely damaged by impact and post-impact fire. Both PT6B-36A engines exhibited rotational scoring on the compressor and turbine sections, indicating that the engines were producing power at the time of impact. The fuel system was compromised, with ruptured lines and a breached fuel tank contributing to the post-impact fire. The Flight Data Recorder (FDR) was recovered from the aft cabin section, approximately 15 feet from the primary impact point. The FDR housing exhibited minor thermal discoloration but remained structurally intact. The unit was transported to the NTSB Vehicle Recorders Laboratory for data extraction and analysis. The MED plug interface was examined and found to be undamaged, with no evidence of electrical shorting or connector separation. The cabin interior was heavily compromised by fire, with seat tracks, overhead panels, and emergency equipment destroyed.

5.3 Recorder Data Recovery
The FDR contained 256 parameters, including altitude, airspeed, heading, vertical speed, engine torque, and control inputs. Data extraction was completed on February 14, 2020, using standard NTSB forensic protocols. The recorded data indicated a steady descent rate of 1,200 feet per minute during the final 45 seconds of flight, with a corresponding increase in collective pitch and engine torque. The aircraft’s heading fluctuated between 035° and 055° magnetic, consistent with a right-turning maneuver. The final recorded altitude was 1,842 feet MSL, with a ground speed of 112 knots. The data was synchronized with radar returns and ATC communications to establish a continuous flight path reconstruction. No anomalies were identified in the FDR data stream prior to impact.

6. ADDITIONAL INFORMATION

6.1 Operator Policies
The operator’s General Operations Manual (GOM) specified weather minimums for VFR flight in mountainous terrain, requiring a minimum visibility of 3 statute miles and a ceiling of 1,000 feet AGL. The manual also mandated that pilots discontinue VFR flight and either divert to an alternate airport or request an IFR clearance if conditions deteriorated below published minimums. The operator’s risk management program included a formal weather decision matrix, which required pilots to consult real-time METAR data, PIREPs, and terrain elevation charts prior to departure. The investigation reviewed the operator’s training syllabus, which included modules on VFR-into-IMC recognition, spatial disorientation mitigation, and emergency descent procedures. No deviations from company policy were documented in the pilot’s pre-flight planning or dispatch records.

6.2 Historical Precedents
The NTSB has investigated multiple accidents involving VFR flight into IMC in mountainous terrain, with similar contributing factors including deteriorating visibility, rising terrain, and delayed decision-making. Notable precedents include the 2015 accident involving a Bell 407 in the Sierra Nevada Mountains and the 2018 accident involving an Airbus AS350 in the Appalachian region. In each case, the investigation documented that pilots continued VFR flight into areas of reduced visibility, resulting in loss of visual reference and spatial disorientation. The NTSB has issued multiple safety recommendations addressing the need for enhanced terrain awareness systems, mandatory IFR training for commercial helicopter operators, and improved weather briefing protocols. The current investigation reviewed these historical findings to identify common operational and environmental factors.

6.3 Regulatory and Advisory Guidance
The operation was subject to 14 CFR 135.181, which requires operators to establish and maintain weather minimums for VFR flight. 14 CFR 135.215 mandates that pilots must not operate under VFR when weather conditions are below the minimums specified in the operator’s manual. Advisory Circular (AC) 91-78, Use of the Cockpit Voice Recorder and Flight Data Recorder, provides guidance on data retention and analysis procedures. AC 90-48C, Pilots’ Role in Collision Avoidance, addresses the importance of maintaining visual reference and avoiding flight into IMC without appropriate certification and equipment. The investigation also referenced 14 CFR Part 25 airworthiness standards for transport category aircraft, which establish environmental qualification requirements for avionics and cabin systems. These standards were reviewed to evaluate the operational resilience of the accident aircraft’s electrical and navigation systems under adverse weather conditions. The NTSB continues to analyze all available data, including maintenance records, pilot training documentation, and meteorological observations, to support the factual record of this investigation.

### [sc-0_witness_testimony_002] Witness Testimony

> **Type:** witness_testimony | **Reliability:** 0.671

INTERVIEW OF DAVID R. CHEN, SENIOR AVIATION OPERATIONS CONSULTANT
Date: February 14, 2020
Time: 09:15 PST
Location: NTSB Field Office, Los Angeles, California
Interviewer: NTSB Investigator M. Torres

Q: Mr. Chen, thank you for coming in today. For the record, could you please state your full name, current occupation, and your relationship to the events of January 26, 2020?

A: Sure. David R. Chen. I’m a senior aviation operations consultant, mostly working with charter operators and maintenance facilities on compliance and safety management systems. I don’t have a direct operational tie to the flight itself, but I was at the Van Nuys FBO that morning doing a routine audit for a client. I was in the operations office, tracking local traffic and weather, when the… when the incident occurred. I was close enough to hear the radio traffic, and I was watching the radar returns on our flight tracking monitors.

Q: Can you walk me through your morning, starting from when you arrived at the facility?

A: Yeah. I got there around, uh, seven-thirty. Maybe seven-forty-five. It was already pretty thick out there. Not like a wall, but… you know, that low, heavy marine layer that just sits in the valleys. I remember looking out the window and the hills were completely washed out. Just gray. I was reviewing some maintenance logs for a Part 135 operator—trying to get their audit squared away before the end of the month. I had my coffee, logged into the flight tracking system, and just… kept an eye on the local departures. The S-76B, I think it was registered as N72EX, filed a VFR flight plan out of John Wayne, heading up to Camarillo, then back down. Standard routing. I didn’t think much of it at first.

Q: At what point did you become aware that something was wrong with that specific flight?

A: Around nine-fifteen, maybe nine-twenty. I was on the phone with a mechanic about a… a MED plug issue on one of our client’s aircraft. You know, the medical oxygen system connector. We were troubleshooting a faulty seal, and I was cross-referencing the maintenance manual. While I was on hold, I glanced at the tracking screen. The helicopter’s ground track was… it was drifting. Not a lot, but it wasn’t following the 101 corridor like it should have. It was pushing north, toward the Santa Monica Mountains. And the altitude readout was… it was holding around a thousand, maybe eleven hundred feet AGL, but the terrain in that area starts climbing fast. I remember thinking, “That’s not a good place to be in this weather.”

Q: Did you hear any radio communications from the pilot or air traffic control?

A: Yeah. I had the local tower and SoCal Approach on the scanner. At first, it was just routine. Then, around nine-thirty, I heard the pilot request a special VFR clearance to continue. He was talking to SoCal, I think. He said something like, “We’re in the soup, but we can see the ground.” And the controller… the controller was hesitant. I remember the exact phrasing because it stuck with me. He said, “Negative, maintain VFR, advise intentions.” The pilot came back, said he was turning around, but… I don’t know. The transmission was choppy. There was a lot of static. And then, maybe two or three minutes later, the track just… stopped updating. The last squawk was still active, but the position froze. I called the FBO manager over, pointed at the screen, and said, “We need to call it in.”

Q: What did you observe visually or audibly from your location at that time?

A: Honestly? Not much. The fog was so dense you couldn’t see past the taxiway lights. But I did hear… I don’t know how to describe it. It wasn’t a crash sound. It was more like… a sudden absence. The rotor wash, the low-frequency thrum that you can feel in your chest when a heavy helicopter is nearby… it just cut off. One second it was there, muffled by the fog, and the next, nothing. Just the wind and the distant hum of the 101. I stepped outside, but you couldn’t see anything. The visibility was maybe a quarter mile, if that. I remember the dampness on my jacket, the smell of wet asphalt and pine from the hills. It felt… heavy.

Q: You mentioned Part 135 earlier. Can you elaborate on your understanding of the regulatory framework that applied to this flight?

A: Right. The operator was flying under 14 CFR Part 135, which governs commuter and on-demand operations. That means they’re held to specific weather minimums, crew training requirements, and operational control standards. VFR into IMC is… it’s a known hazard. The regulations are pretty clear about it. You don’t continue into instrument conditions unless you’re instrument-rated, the aircraft is equipped for it, and you’re on an IFR clearance. I’ve seen it happen before, not with this operator, but in the industry generally. Pilots get pressure, they get spatial disorientation, they think they can thread the needle. But the mountains don’t care. And when you’re in a rotorcraft, the margin for error shrinks fast.

Q: How does this compare to the certification standards you’re familiar with in fixed-wing transport operations?

A: Completely different world. I spent twelve years at Southwest Airlines before I went independent. We were dealing with 737s, CFM56-7B engines, full glass cockpits, redundant systems, strict dispatch release procedures. That’s 14 CFR Part 25 territory. Transport category aircraft are built to survive a lot more, and the operational oversight is… it’s just more rigid. You don’t get a Part 25 jet flying into a mountain pass in fog without an IFR clearance and a full weather briefing. The S-76B is a fantastic machine, don’t get me wrong, but it’s not certified to the same structural and systems redundancy standards. It’s a different risk profile. And when you mix that with a pilot who’s trying to maintain visual reference in deteriorating conditions… it’s a recipe for what happened.

Q: Were you aware of any FAA oversight or inspection activities related to this operator prior to the accident?

A: I wasn’t directly involved, but I know the FAA had been conducting ramp checks in the area. Order 8900.1 outlines the Flight Standards Information Management System, and it’s pretty clear on how inspectors are supposed to evaluate Part 135 operators for weather minimum compliance and pilot decision-making. I’ve read through the guidance. It’s thorough. But the reality on the ground… it’s hard to catch a single flight decision in real time. You can audit logs, you can check training records, but you can’t sit in the cockpit when the ceiling drops. The FAA does what it can, but the final call is always the pilot’s. And in this case, the call was wrong.

Q: After the track disappeared, what did you do?

A: I called 911 first. Gave them the last known coordinates, the registration, the time. Then I contacted the NTSB duty officer through our company’s emergency protocol. I stayed on site until local law enforcement and fire arrived. I didn’t go to the crash site. I knew better than to interfere with the scene. I just… I sat in the operations room, watched the news feeds, and waited for the investigators to show up. It was a long day.

Q: Is there anything else you’d like to add for the record?

A: Just… I’ve been in aviation for twenty-three years. I’ve seen close calls, I’ve seen maintenance errors, I’ve seen weather win. But this one… it’s hard to shake. You look at the data, you read the weather reports, you listen to the audio, and it’s just… it’s preventable. The pilot was experienced, the aircraft was airworthy, but the environment changed faster than the decision loop could adapt. I hope the NTSB’s findings help push better training, better weather decision-making tools, maybe even stricter operational controls for VFR flights in marginal conditions. That’s all I can say. I’m here to help however I can.

Q: Thank you, Mr. Chen. We’ll be in touch if we need clarification.

A: Of course. Thank you.

1. ACCIDENT INFORMATION

1.1 Date, Time, and Location
On April 17, 2018, at approximately 1543:12 Eastern Daylight Time (EDT), an uncontained engine failure occurred aboard a Boeing 737-700 while operating in cruise flight at 32,500 feet mean sea level (MSL). The event transpired over Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, at geographic coordinates 40.012° N, 76.184° W. Following the failure, the flight crew initiated an emergency descent and diverted to Philadelphia International Airport (KPHL), where the aircraft landed safely at 1603:45 EDT. The rapid decompression resulting from the event caused one passenger fatality and multiple injuries, as documented in the operator’s passenger manifest and emergency medical response logs.

1.2 Aircraft Identification and Operator
The aircraft involved was a Boeing 737-7H4, manufacturer serial number 28748, bearing U.S. registration N772SW. The aircraft was operated by Southwest Airlines (SWA), a certificated air carrier holding Air Carrier Certificate number SWAA001A. The operator conducted the flight under the authority of Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations (14 CFR) Part 121. The aircraft was configured in a single-class layout with 143 passenger seats and was equipped with winglets, a blended winglet modification incorporated in 2012.

1.3 Nature of Flight and Regulatory Framework
The flight was designated as SWA 1380, a scheduled domestic passenger service departing from LaGuardia Airport (KLGA), New York, with a planned destination of Dallas Love Field (KDAL), Texas. The flight was conducted in accordance with the operator’s FAA-approved Operations Specifications, specifically OpsSpec A001 (Authorization to Conduct Operations Under Part 121) and OpsSpec A008 (Aircraft Flight Manual and Weight and Balance). All operational parameters, including crew duty periods, fuel planning, and dispatch release, were documented and verified prior to departure in compliance with 14 CFR § 121.533 and § 121.595.

2. CREW INFORMATION

2.1 Captain Qualifications and Certifications

A first-class medical certificate was issued on January 12, 2018, with no limitations or waivers. Total flight time was recorded at 11,842 hours, of which 8,920 hours were accumulated in the Boeing 737 series. 2.2 First Officer Qualifications and Certifications
The first officer held an ATP certificate, number 7654321, with multi-engine land and Boeing 737 type ratings. A first-class medical certificate was issued on December 20, 2017, without restrictions. Total flight time was 7,640 hours, with 3,115 hours in the Boeing 737. The first officer completed recurrent training, including line-oriented flight training (LOFT) and emergency procedure drills, on January 22, 2018. Both pilots were within the 90-day recency requirements for takeoffs and landings as mandated by 14 CFR § 121.439. The captain had accumulated 142 flight hours in the preceding 90 days, while the first officer had logged 118 hours. Training records indicated completion of the operator’s standard upset prevention and recovery training (UPRT) module, which included simulated rapid decompression, emergency descent, and single-engine drift-down procedures. The operator’s training syllabus incorporated scenario-based instruction for uncontained engine failures, emphasizing immediate crew coordination, oxygen mask deployment, and adherence to Quick Reference Handbook (QRH) memory items. No discrepancies were noted in the pilots’ qualification files, recent line operational safety audits (LOSA), or voluntary safety reports.

3. AIRCRAFT INFORMATION

3.1 Airframe Specifications and History
The Boeing 737-7H4 was manufactured in 1998 and delivered to Southwest Airlines on May 14, 1998. At the time of the accident, the airframe had accumulated 57,420 total flight hours and 38,110 flight cycles. The aircraft was equipped with CFM International CFM56-7B24E turbofan engines. The airframe maintenance program was conducted under an FAA-approved continuous airworthiness maintenance program (CAMP) in accordance with 14 CFR § 121.369. The aircraft’s weight and balance documentation indicated a takeoff weight of 132,450 pounds, with a center of gravity at 24.1% mean aerodynamic chord (MAC).

3.2 Engine Specifications and Maintenance
The left engine, serial number 683042, was a CFM56-7B24E rated at 24,200 pounds of thrust. It was installed on the accident aircraft on August 12, 2016, and had accumulated 12,450 flight hours and 8,920 cycles since new. The engine was maintained per the manufacturer’s maintenance planning document (MPD) and the operator’s reliability program. Engine condition monitoring system (ECMS) data indicated stable exhaust gas temperature (EGT) margins and normal oil consumption trends throughout the preceding 1,200 flight hours.

3.3 Relevant Systems and Components
The aircraft was equipped with a digital flight data acquisition unit (DFDAU) interfaced with a solid-state Flight Data Recorder (FDR), capable of recording 25 hours of operational parameters. The recorder was manufactured by L-3 Communications and met Technical Standard Order (TSO) C124a requirements. The cabin pressurization system utilized dual outflow valves and was controlled by the cabin pressure controller (CPC). The emergency oxygen system consisted of chemical oxygen generators deployed automatically upon cabin altitude exceeding 14,000 feet. The hydraulic system operated at 3,000 psi, with system A and system B providing redundancy for flight control actuation.

3.4 Applicable Airworthiness Directives and Service Bulletins
At the time of the event, the aircraft was in compliance with all applicable Airworthiness Directives (ADs), including AD 2016-08-09, which mandated ultrasonic inspections of CFM56-7B fan blades for fatigue cracking. The operator’s maintenance tracking system indicated that the left engine’s fan blades had undergone the required eddy current and ultrasonic inspections on April 2, 2018, with no anomalies reported. Service Bulletin CFM56-7B S/B 72-0823 had been incorporated during the 2017 shop visit. All maintenance logbook entries were reviewed and found to be properly signed, dated, and compliant with 14 CFR § 121.380.

4. METEOROLOGICAL INFORMATION

On April 17, 2018, a high-pressure system dominated the northeastern United States, resulting in generally clear skies and stable atmospheric conditions. Ground impact marks were consistent with high-velocity debris penetration of agricultural fields and residential structures, with no evidence of post-impact fire.

5.2 Engine Examination Findings
Post-accident examination of the left engine revealed a catastrophic failure of the No. 13 fan blade. The blade exhibited fatigue cracking originating from a subsurface inclusion near the dovetail root. The fracture surface displayed characteristic beach marks and a final fast-fracture zone. The failure resulted in the release of high-energy debris that penetrated the engine inlet cowl, severed hydraulic lines, and impacted the left fuselage. The right engine remained undamaged and operated normally throughout the emergency descent and landing. Engine mount attachments were inspected and found structurally sound, with no evidence of secondary structural failure.

5.3 Fuselage and Cabin Window Damage
Debris impact caused a breach in the left fuselage skin at station 1040, frame 12, measuring approximately 18 inches by 12 inches. The breach resulted in rapid decompression of the passenger cabin. A cabin window at row 14, seat A, was dislodged due to the pressure differential and structural deformation. The window frame exhibited plastic deformation consistent with outward explosive loading. The emergency oxygen masks deployed automatically, and the cabin altitude warning activated at 14,200 feet. The rapid decompression and window dislodgement resulted in one passenger fatality and multiple injuries, as documented in the operator’s passenger manifest and emergency medical response logs.

5.4 Flight Data Recorder and Cockpit Voice Recorder Analysis
The solid-state Flight Data Recorder (FDR) was recovered from the aft equipment bay and downloaded without data corruption. The recorder captured 256 parameters at a sampling rate of 1 Hz. Data indicated a sudden loss of left engine N1 and N2 parameters at 1543:12 EDT, coinciding with a rapid increase in cabin altitude from 6,800 feet to 14,500 feet within 4 seconds. The aircraft initiated an emergency descent at 1543:18 EDT, descending at a maximum rate of 6,200 feet per minute. The Cockpit Voice Recorder (CVR) captured crew communications, warning chimes, and ambient noise consistent with rapid decompression. Both recorders were secured and forwarded to the NTSB Vehicle Recorders Laboratory for detailed analysis. The FDR data was cross-referenced with the operator’s engine condition monitoring system (ECMS) to validate pre-event performance baselines.

6. ADDITIONAL INFORMATION

### [sc-1_technical_analysis_004] Technical Analysis

> **Type:** technical_analysis | **Reliability:** 0.809

1. INTRODUCTION
This Specialist’s Factual Report documents the examination, testing, and analysis of the left engine assembly, recovered structural components, and flight data recorder (FDR) parameters associated with the uncontained engine failure event involving a Boeing 737-700, registration N772SW, operated by Southwest Airlines (SWA). The investigation was conducted under the authority of the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) Powerplants and Structures Groups. The scope of this examination encompassed the physical and metallurgical evaluation of the CFM56-7B26 turbofan engine, specifically the number 13 fan blade assembly, the titanium containment ring, the forward fuselage skin panel, the affected cabin window frame, and the Main Entry Door (MED) plug assembly. Additionally, digital flight data recorder (DFDR) parameters were extracted, decoded, and analyzed to reconstruct the temporal sequence of the failure event and correlate mechanical signatures with aerodynamic and pressurization responses.

The physical examination of the engine components and recovered debris was performed between May 14 and May 28, 2018, at the NTSB Materials Laboratory in Washington, D.C., and the CFM International teardown facility in Cincinnati, Ohio. The DFDR data was processed and analyzed at the NTSB Vehicle Recorders Laboratory from June 2 to June 10, 2018. All examinations were conducted in accordance with NTSB investigative protocols, ASTM International standards, SAE Aerospace Material Specifications (AMS), and applicable Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) advisory circulars.

2. BACKGROUND
The subject aircraft was equipped with three CFM56-7B26 high-bypass turbofan engines, manufactured under a joint venture between General Electric and Safran Aircraft Engines. The left engine, serial number 712485, was installed on the aircraft on March 12, 2014, following a performance restoration shop visit conducted by Atlas Air Worldwide Holdings (AAR) maintenance facilities. At the time of the event, the engine had accumulated 32,115 flight cycles and 45,892.3 flight hours. The engine is certified under 14 CFR Part 33, with the fan blade containment system designed to meet §33.75 requirements for uncontained failure scenarios and §33.76 for rotor burst testing.

The fan rotor assembly consists of 24 wide-chord, hollow titanium fan blades, manufactured from Ti-6Al-4V alloy per AMS 4928 specifications. The subject blade, part number 1730M52G01, serial number 004821, was installed at position 13 (clockwise from top dead center) during the 2014 shop visit. The blade design incorporates a mid-span shroud and a dovetail root interface with a titanium alloy disk. Fatigue life management for the CFM56-7B series is governed by FAA Airworthiness Directive 2016-11-09, which mandates periodic eddy current inspections of the fan blade airfoil and root regions. The last compliant inspection was performed on February 18, 2018, at 31,980 cycles, with no anomalies reported.

The containment ring, fabricated from Inconel 718 per AMS 5663, is designed to absorb and contain fan blade fragments up to a specified kinetic energy threshold. The forward fuselage skin is constructed from 2024-T3 aluminum alloy, with a nominal thickness of 0.050 inches. The affected cabin window assembly utilizes a three-pane acrylic/polycarbonate laminate system, certified to 14 CFR §25.773 for pressure retention and impact resistance. The MED plug, a structural door panel assembly, incorporates a pressure-equalization vent and a multi-point latching mechanism designed to maintain cabin pressurization integrity. Industry fatigue life prediction methodologies, including those historically developed by Rolls-Royce (RR) for rotating titanium components, were referenced for comparative crack growth modeling, though the CFM56-7B utilizes proprietary GE/Safran life-limiting criteria.

3. EXAMINATION AND FINDINGS
The physical examination commenced with a macroscopic inspection of the recovered fan blade fragment, which represented approximately 62% of the original airfoil geometry. The fracture surface exhibited distinct regions of fatigue crack propagation and final ductile overload. The fatigue origin was located 1.85 inches from the leading edge and 0.42 inches below the airfoil surface, consistent with a subsurface initiation site. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at 500x magnification revealed a cluster of non-metallic inclusions, primarily aluminum oxide (Al2O3) and titanium nitride (TiN), measuring 0.008 to 0.012 inches in diameter. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed the inclusion chemistry, which exceeded the maximum allowable limits per AMS 4928 for clean titanium forgings.

Fatigue striation spacing was measured across the propagation zone using SEM at 2000x magnification. The average striation spacing increased from 0.00004 inches near the origin to 0.00031 inches at the transition to the final fracture zone, indicating progressive crack growth under cyclic loading. Application of the Paris Law (da/dN = C(ΔK)^m) with material constants for Ti-6Al-4V (C = 1.2×10^-11, m = 3.8) yielded an estimated crack propagation life of 1,180 ± 150 cycles. The final overload region exhibited 45° shear lips and dimpled rupture morphology, characteristic of tensile failure at high rotational speeds.

Hardness testing was performed on the blade root and airfoil sections using a Rockwell C scale indenter. Measured values ranged from 38.2 to 39.8 HRC, consistent with the specified heat treatment condition (solution treated and aged). Microstructural analysis per ASTM E112 revealed a bimodal alpha-beta grain structure with an average grain size of 8.5 μm, within the acceptable range for AMS 4928. No evidence of thermal degradation, hydrogen embrittlement, or foreign object damage (FOD) was observed.

The titanium containment ring exhibited localized plastic deformation and a through-thickness penetration measuring 4.2 inches in length and 1.8 inches in width. The impact zone showed radial tearing and delamination of the composite backing layer. Tensile coupon testing of the ring material per ASTM E8 indicated a yield strength of 1,180 MPa and ultimate tensile strength of 1,340 MPa, confirming material compliance. However, finite element analysis (FEA) of the impact event, using recovered fragment mass (14.2 lbs) and estimated rotational velocity (3,850 RPM), calculated a kinetic energy of 1.85×10^6 ft-lbs, exceeding the design containment threshold of 1.42×10^6 ft-lbs by 30.3%.

The forward fuselage skin panel sustained a 12.5-inch longitudinal tear and a 6.8-inch transverse puncture. The 2024-T3 aluminum exhibited strain hardening at the fracture edges, with hardness values increasing from 125 HB to 148 HB. The affected cabin window frame showed severe plastic deformation, with the outer acrylic pane completely fractured and the inner polycarbonate layer exhibiting craze cracking. The window retention clips were sheared at the mounting bolts, which failed in double shear at an estimated load of 4,200 lbs per fastener.

The MED plug assembly was examined for structural integrity and pressure retention capability. The plug skin exhibited localized buckling and fastener pull-through at stations 142.5 and 145.0. The pressure-equalization vent mechanism was found in the open position, with the diaphragm ruptured at 11.2 PSI differential pressure. The multi-point latching system showed no evidence of pre-existing wear or misalignment. All fasteners were torqued to manufacturer specifications (45 ± 5 ft-lbs) at the time of examination.

4. DATA ANALYSIS
Digital flight data recorder (DFDR) parameters were extracted from the solid-state memory module using a certified readout system compliant with ARINC 717 standards. The data was sampled at 1 Hz for engine parameters and 4 Hz for flight control and cabin pressure parameters. The following timeline reconstruction is based on synchronized UTC timestamps:

At 10:43:27.0 UTC, the aircraft was in cruise flight at 32,500 ft MSL, with an indicated airspeed of 285 knots and a cabin differential pressure of 8.15 PSI. Engine parameters were within normal operating envelopes: N1 at 91.8%, N2 at 94.2%, EGT at 648°C, and fuel flow at 2,840 lbs/hr. Vibration monitoring (VIB) indicated a baseline level of 0.12 inches/sec.

At 10:43:27.4 UTC, a transient N1 vibration spike of 1.85 inches/sec was recorded, coinciding with a 0.3% N1 decay and a 12°C EGT increase. This signature is consistent with initial fan blade fracture and rotor imbalance. At 10:43:28.1 UTC, N1 rapidly decreased to 78.4%, while EGT peaked at 712°C. The engine vibration parameter exceeded the DFDR recording limit of 2.0 inches/sec, indicating severe rotor unbalance.

At 10:43:28.9 UTC, cabin differential pressure dropped from 8.15 PSI to 2.4 PSI within 0.8 seconds, indicating rapid decompression. The cabin altitude parameter increased from 6,800 ft to 24,500 ft over a 3.2-second interval. The aircraft initiated an emergency descent, with vertical speed reaching -4,200 ft/min. The autothrottle system commanded thrust reduction, and the left engine N1 stabilized at 42.1% as the engine control unit (ECU) entered a degraded mode.

At 10:43:31.5 UTC, the right engine N1 increased to 96.5% to maintain thrust asymmetry compensation. The aircraft bank angle reached 18° left, consistent with asymmetric thrust and aerodynamic drag from the damaged nacelle. The FDR recorded a transient yaw rate of 4.2°/sec, which was corrected by rudder input.

Comparison with normal operational parameters from the aircraft’s Quick Access Recorder (QAR) database indicates that the pre-failure engine health monitoring (EHM) trends showed no significant deviations. The last 500 flight cycles exhibited N1 vibration levels averaging 0.14 ± 0.03 inches/sec, with EGT margins within 15°C of baseline. The sudden onset of vibration and pressure decay confirms an uncontained, high-energy failure event with no prior detectable degradation in the FDR parameters.

5. SUMMARY
The examination and analysis of the left engine assembly, recovered debris, and flight data recorder parameters yielded the following factual findings:

1. The number 13 fan blade (P/N 1730M52G01, S/N 004821) failed due to high-cycle fatigue originating from a subsurface cluster of non-metallic inclusions (Al2O3/TiN) that exceeded AMS 4928 chemical purity specifications.
2. Fatigue crack propagation occurred over an estimated 1,180 ± 150 flight cycles, with striation spacing consistent with progressive growth under cyclic centrifugal and aerodynamic loading.
3. The final fracture occurred at cruise conditions (32,500 ft MSL, 285 KIAS, N1 91.8%), resulting in an uncontained release of a 14.2 lb blade fragment.
4. The titanium containment ring sustained a through-thickness penetration, with calculated impact energy (1.85×10^6 ft-lbs) exceeding the certified design threshold by 30.3%.
5. The forward fuselage skin and cabin window assembly sustained impact damage consistent with high-velocity debris penetration, resulting in rapid cabin decompression (8.15 PSI to 2.4 PSI in 0.8 seconds).
6. The MED plug assembly exhibited localized structural deformation and fastener pull-through, with the pressure-equalization vent diaphragm rupturing at 11.2 PSI differential pressure.
7. DFDR data confirms a sudden onset of rotor imbalance, N1 decay, EGT rise, and cabin pressure loss, with no prior detectable anomalies in engine health monitoring parameters.
8. Material testing of the fan blade, containment ring, and fuselage components confirmed compliance with applicable AMS, ASTM, and FAA certification standards, with the exception of the subsurface inclusion content in the failed blade.

All findings are presented as factual observations derived from physical examination, metallurgical analysis, and flight data reconstruction. No determination of probable cause is included in this report.
